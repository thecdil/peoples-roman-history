Roman gladiatorial games are often remembered as chaotic spectacles of violence, but in reality, they were carefully controlled performances shaped by figures like the *lanista.*  The lanista’s authority extended over most aspects of a gladiator’s life.  These ideas become clearer when viewed through the life of Gaius Lartius Priscus, a fictional character based on scholarly research. His life reflects what is known about gladiators and *lanista* in the Roman Empire. Through tellings of what is known about the system of power and control that defined Roman spectacle.  

{% include essay/image.html objectid="Wheatley_001" %}

Gaius Lartius Priscus, like a few other lanista, was of freedman status.[^1] In his early life, he was believed to have fought as a gladiator at a colosseum in the city of Bovianum. After being freed from servitude through his success as a gladiator, he is believed to have moved to Capua, a city well known for its gladiatorial schools or *ludus*.[^2] The ludus in this area was known as Neronian Training schools, meaning they were created during the Neronian era 54-68 CE. [^3]

## Infamia and Social Stigma

{% include essay/image.html objectid="Wheatley_002" %}

Although viewed better than gladiators, *lanista* was still believed to be *infames.*[^4] * Infamia* was a social status that was associated with people who carried immoral or “un-Roman” professions. Any profession where one would use their body as a tool for their work would fall under *infamia*. Examples of these jobs include pimps, actors, gladiators, *lanista*, etc. *Lanista* are believed to fall under this demographic because they bought and sold people. This status was also given to those that were convicted criminals. Gladiators were also marked as *infamia* because they performed much like actors. In addition, gladiators were often either criminals or slaves.  The term *infamia* shows the importance of status and reputation within Roman society. For Gaius Lartius Priscus, this meant that even after transitioning to a *lanista*, his identity remained socially marked and limited.  

## The Systematic Organization of Gladiatorial Combat

Evidence of the first *munera*, or gladiatorial combat, appeared in 264 BC.[^5] In the beginning, many gladiatorial bouts were believed to be performed as part of funeral arrangements. Romans believed that blood spilt during these funerary arrangements would help the passage of its leaders.[^6] During the start of the Roman Empire, the purpose of these bouts shifted. It is believed that the combats began to represent the central authority of Augustus and helped legitimize his rule. This continued to transform over the course of the empire, and the gladiator became a symbol of idealized military power.

Within the conflicts there were different gladiator types that would appear, such as the *Murmillo*, *Retiarius*, *Secutor*, and *Thraex.*   Each of the classes would fight with different weapons and were provided with different armor. For example, the *Murmillo* possessed a heavy shield and a fish shaped helmet, but the *Retiarius* has a net, trident and light armor. ## Hierarchy and Control in the Roman Arena

The transition of gladiator to lanista shows how control was embedded within spectacles performed in the Colosseum. There is a clear hierarchy of those that had power within the colosseum and what the spectacles were transformed to represent. Those that fought within the gladiatorial bouts were at the bottom, most of them being criminals and *infamia*. Even the next layer up, *lanista*, was not viewed much better still being labeled as *infamia*. Although the *lanista* were able to have control over groups of gladiators or familia.[^8] *Lanista* would train and dictate most of the daily life of their familia. For the games to take place, gladiators would be rented out to the *editores*. These individuals were believed to be local elites that were responsible for funding and organizing the games*. *In addition to funding the games, they often would own the *ludus* that the gladiators would train at.* Editores *were also able to determine the outcome of a draw within the bouts.

## Roman Spectacle as a System of Power

Gaius Lartius Priscus' story helps display that Roman spectacle was not just entertainment, but a structured system of power that helped shape society. Enabling leaders to show their control to those in the arena. *Editores* could show this by having lavish bouts for those attending to watch or by determining the outcome of the conflicts. The aspect of gladiator and lanista relationship shows how a certain class can have control over a group of people. The matchups of fighters also point to being heavily controlled to potentially favor one outcome or another. All of this information helps highlight how spectacles were used as a form of control within the Roman Empire.
