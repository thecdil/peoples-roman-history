Sixteen percent of the world's population today is disabled.[^1] With laws, inventions (tools) and even culture and language being impacted by it. Yet accessibility and the laws like Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) we know today have only been in effect for less than half a century. Disability, however, has always been around.

We must look closer into history not just to better understand the present and how we have progressed, but also to try to dig deeper into what stories (and people) are forgotten. Let’s use Romans as a case study in understanding what disability was like in the ancient world. This is an essay about Aurelia Chreste, a fictional woman living in Ostia, Italy during the imperial Rome era. By talking about disabilities through Aurelia, we gain a closer understanding of what it was like to experience/ live in imperial Rome and are able to relate to the human and important social issue of disability that impacts all time periods.

## Introduction 

{% include essay/video-modal.html objectid="Craig_001" heading_level="h3" %}

The first thing we will explore is how disability impacts citizenship. One’s social status determined how one was treated. While the rich and citizens had more rights and could afford more things like mobility aids and health care, the poor and enslaved could be treated like monsters, lacking proper resources. Rome and other major cities had “Monster markets” where slaves would be sold specifically for their disabilities. Some slaves were even badly injured for the sake of selling them at market for a higher price as “monsters”. Disability was sought after as means of entertainment and related to magic and myth. Dwarfism was seen in elite circles of Roman society for amusement or as advisers. Dwarfism was also seen in folklore as mythical people in Rome, increasing the fascination with those who had it. Other physical disabilities were often also tied to moral failing or seen as a punishment from the gods.[^2] like blindness being due to have strong powers of prophecy.

Yet becoming disabled was not always seen as a negative or exotic. A soldier could be discharged from the Roman army for a severe injury (called *Missio Causaria*) was considered honorable.[^3] These injuries could range from blindness, which many generals or commanders were praised for, to a missing limb or mental disability from trauma. These injuries often had long term effects and impacted one’s standard of living even outside of the army, making them disabled.

As a citizen, Aurelia was one of the lucky few disabled. In Rome (as opposed to places like Greece) women despite physical disability, one had the ability to inherit from their father and even could own the property and could help manage her family’s wool business.[^4] However, women were excluded from political positions and would most likely have a guardian (like her uncle or male in her extended family) as she no longer had a father and was not married and had legal power over her.Those who were higher class not only had rights as citizens but could also afford not to work as laborious jobs or choose their profession, while those in the middle and lower class would still be expected to work despite the hardship and discomfort disability often caused. One example of elite privilege was Quintus Pedius[^5], who was one of the first people recorded to be deaf. Because of his family's elite position and status (his father was a senator) he was able to still have a job and place in society as a painter. In fact the writer, Pliny the Elder, even praises Quintus’ talent.

## The Deaf and The Blind 

{% include essay/image.html objectid="Craig_002" caption="Figure 2: A carved marble tablet depicting two ears in the center" %}

Latin inscription: *“Cutius Gallus once vowed these ears to you, offspring of Phoebus, now being healthy as regards \[his\] ears”*[^3]

The inscription above mentions the “offspring of Phoebus” which refers to Asklepios, a god often related to medicine or healing. This gives us a glimpse on illness and physical ailments that were thought to be treated and healed by more than just medicine. This also gives insight into which gods and acts of religion or beliefs were seen in daily life.

This tablet, along with other anatomical Votives, (a clay or marble sculpture often depicting body parts, that gives thanks for or ask for healing from the gods) could be found at places like the Sanctuary of Asklepius or Temple of Aesculapius as thanks or request to the Roman gods for healing.This shows that the Romans combined medicine with magic and religion.The intertwining of magic can also be seen linked with disabilities when it comes to theories on what caused it. Blindness was often thought to be due to an “evil eye” or curse (caused by looking at someone with a malicious glare) placed upon them. Pendants or amulets were often worn for protection.[^7]

Unfortunately not all disabilities were treated the same. Blind often had more rights and citizenship than those deaf and mute. The deaf could not hold office in the senate or speak in court, while we have evidence of blind senators holding positions even after becoming blind. Speaking was a key part of what it meant to be a “true” Roman citizen causing the deaf and/or mute to be excluded from parts of citizenship. However, becoming deaf did not mean a total loss. If a soldier becomes deaf or hard of hearing during his time served, there is a possibility of continuing in the military. And while trading and business deals were often required verbal transactions making it difficult to communicate figure spelling or symbols was common even among the non-deaf.[^8]

## Mobility: travel and in daily life 

{% include essay/image.html objectid="Craig_003" %}

The image is a replica of the “Capua Limb”, the only artificial limb known to have survived from the ancient Greece and Rome period. A Roman prosthetic right leg found in a tomb in Capua Italy. The original “Capua Limb” was made of wood and bronze.

Cost and distance weren’t the only thing Aurelia had to worry about while traveling. Being deaf might lead to more vulnerability when traveling and even reduce how she can travel. We don’t know when Aurelia became deaf (birth or later in life) and how she communicated. However, the evidence allows us to imagine that she must likely spoke Latin, Greek, or possibly even sign language or hand signals were her main way of communicating. Most of those in the Italian region of the Roman Empire spoke Latin or in the periods Greek. And if she was also mute or born, deaf hand signals would be necessary to express needs or emotions, especially within her extended family. Ancient Rome used some hand signals to communicate during trading and written text during the time describes death family members communicating with each other and within their small communities.[^9] The evidence also allows us to imagine that she would rely on a hearing member of her family or a guardian to help with communication and negotiating trade and travel fees as she was required to have a guardian even when traveling.[^10] for those disabled like Aurelia.

Even going to the next large city, like the capital of Rome, which was 28 kilometers away from Ostia, would be a difficult journey. Taking an average 2.9 days by wagon or donkey to get there now has it’s challenges.For those who had a mobility-related disability,like a prosthetic leg, travel would be even more challenging and would likely be restricted to wagons or carriages to allow for comfort. As traveling can cause physical conditions to worsen or strain a healthy body let alone one disabled. Raising the cost of travel reduces and constrains one’s ability to travel, including how often, and even the duration and distance at a time. Someone with neurodivergence and mental disability might also deal with overstimulation, executive function, difficulties with social situations, and many other things when traveling as well. Some may never even travel away from their hometowns due to their disability as well.[^11] However, it is important to note that we may never know how disabilities truly impacted people like Aurelia Chreste.

## Disability ingrained into Roman culture 

{% include essay/image.html objectid="Craig_004" caption="Figure 4: A sculpture cast in leaded bronze depicting the Roman- Egyptian God Harpocrates" %}

Disabilities can be seen throughout Roman history, hidden in pain in sight. Through sculptures, coins (depictions of emperors and gods), art, stories and myths, and even with family members or friends could be seen in daily life. One such example is Harpocrates (called Horus the child in Egypt or the god of silence and secrecy by the Romans) which is shown above. Harpocrates is thought to have what we call today cerebral palsy.[^12]

Another Example was Vulcan (or Hephaestus in Greece). One of his legs is severely injured when is young (born with a limb difference and further worsened by his mother Hera throwing him out of Olympus).[^13] He is seen to always sitting or with a mobile aid (like a donkey or cane/staff) and can even at times be seen with one foot being deformed in some Roman and Greek art.

## Why Aurelia Chreste’s Story Matters Today

Stories like Aurelia Chreste are still important today. Unfortunately views from ancient Roman times can still be seen today on which disabilities have value or are respectable. And One’s value or worth is determined by what one can do or how “disabled” they are. The “Golden” standard of what the human body should look like, is also deeply ingrained in today’s society and social perception. Often ignoring many minorities or the reality of diversity including disability, gender or race being vast. This stems from ancient roman times of what it means to be an “ideal roman” or true roman citizen.

Translations and descriptions from primary sources during imperial Rome used words like “stupid” or “insane:” to cover both physical and mental disabilities and were very generalized.Today we've gotten more specific and differentiated. Ancient Roman text also used “infirmus, deformis, monstrum and lusus naturae” meaning monster, deformed, etc which disabled people were labeled as now have different social meanings and are no longer used.

Some also assume that there was no representation of disability in imperial Rome because children killed by law due to it (called infanticide) must mean that they didn’t exist. And that disability was viewed only negatively, however this is untrue. Disability could be formed at any age for illness or injury,and many disabled children lived into adulthood. As was talked about above, disability was not always considered negative and its members often were an important part of the community (even accepted or considered a blessing or sign at times).

Some good came from the classic ages as well. Ramps and mobility aids like prosthetics, were invented thanks to ancient Rome and Greece. These tools helped shape disability accessibility and technology that we use today.

It is important to remember that inequality is not so simple and often ties together multiple aspects besides just disability. Things like wealth, location, access to resources, and social perception, etc. all impact each other. Disability in the ancient world was much more present than we think, from all walks of life from rich to poor and is deeply ingrained in the culture and stories we hear about Rome (and other classical periods).
