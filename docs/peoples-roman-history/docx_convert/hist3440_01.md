Title: Masculus and Power, Empire, and Resistance in the Roman Empire

Author Email: Burr5615@vandals.uidaho.edu

Author: Hunter Burrows

Multi-Modal Essay-Masculus and Power, Empire, and Resistance in the Roman Empire

Character: Masculus, age 30, freeborn provincial cavalry officer. Heliopolis (Baalbek), Syria, 66 AD.

Theme: Empire, Power, and Resistance

When people think of Ancient Rome and its military, they usually think of the large battles (primarily in the Republican Period), the infantry, or the Emperors and Generals who led campaigns. Rarely is there a focus on the smaller pieces, such as the individual soldier. Even less so is life on the Roman frontier, or the resistance and revolts that arose. It is true that there is less written evidence for those things, and they are even more seldom shown in popular culture and media.

However, the importance of the individual soldier like Masculus, a plausible fictional character created using reconstructed evidence, can not be understated in the shaping of the history of Rome and the world, and those stories need to be told.

**Symbols of Power on the Frontiers**

<img src="media/image3.png" style="width:4.2181in;height:3.16357in" />

Figure 1: Reading Museum, UK \[Silchester Eagle\], [<u>Carole Raddato</u>](https://www.flickr.com/people/41523983@N08), [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reading_Museum_(7690279788).jpg#Licensing), [<u>CC ASA 2.0</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/deed.en)

Roman eagles, known as *aquila,* when on the battlefield and on the frontiers were symbols of Roman power. Although not as commonly displayed as the modern media would suggest, these *aquila*, like the Silchester Eagle pictured above and found in Roman Britain, were often carried and displayed in Roman forts. These held an important religious connotation too, being seen as symbols of the legion and protection from Jupiter. Being a standard bearer in the Roman Army with an *aquila* was seen as a great honor[^1] by men like Masculus. This religious importance is the reason why when a standard was lost in Parthia in 53 BC, Augustus Caesar worked so hard to get it returned in 20 BC and praised himself for having done so.[^2]

> <img src="media/image1.png" style="width:4.42188in;height:3.45105in" />

Figure 2: Reconstruction of Saalburg Roman Fort, [<u>Carole Raddato</u>](https://www.flickr.com/people/41523983@N08), [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reading_Museum_(7690279788).jpg#Licensing), [<u>CC ASA 2.0</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/deed.en)

Forts were another well-known symbol of the might of Imperial Rome. Masculus would have lived in, and therefore encountered, these forts on a daily basis. Part of the reason the forts were such important symbols of power in Ancient Rome was because they controlled movement in and out of the Empire and preventing raiding.[^3] A merchant moving from Parthia into Rome would know they had reached the mighty, powerful Roman Empire by passing by these fortresses, generally built along roads and trade routes.[^4] Masculus’ whole life would have revolved around protecting these routes.

**Fortifications and Camp Life on the Frontiers**

> <img src="media/image9.png" style="width:4.44271in;height:3.33203in" />

Figure 3: Cohort Fort Saalburg, [<u>Holger Weinandt</u>](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benutzer:Schaengel), [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saalburg_-_Haupteingang_2009.jpg), [<u>CC ASA 3.0 Germany</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/de/deed.en)

But what were these Roman forts, and what was life in them like? Roman forts, like the one pictured above, would have been built out of local materials, in this case stone: in Syria, Masculus would have likely lived in a limestone and mud-bricks fort.[^5] These forts would be built by the soldiers, and generally would feature large ditches outside the walls anywhere between 9-12 feet wide and 7-9 feet deep.[^6] Large watchtowers would guard the gates.

At the center of the fort, there was something known as the *praetorium.* The *praetorium* would be a large square, making up the headquarters of the fort. To either side of this area were the arsenal, treasury, markets, camp exchange, commissary, tribunal, and sacrificial altar.[^7] The sacrificial altar again ties in the very religious nature of the Roman army. These areas would have been tied into the daily life of Masculus. If he needed to purchase something, he would likely go off to the market. If Masculus was in need of food he could go bake himself some bread. If Masculus was in need of religious guidance, he could possibly go to the various religious pieces within the camp.

> <img src="media/image4.png" style="width:3.60938in;height:3.52939in" />

Figure 4: Hardknott Roman Fort, Social England, [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hardknott_Roman_Fort.png), [<u>PD-US</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Template:PD-US)

Within the Roman fort, there were barracks for the living of the average soldier. But cavalrymen like Masculus would have a different living situation. Within the Roman Forts, the cavalry would live in “Stable-Barracks.” These would be exactly how they sound: a mix of normal barracks living for the cavalrymen and the stables for their horses, all living in the same building.[^8] As part of Masculus’ daily work, he along with other cavalrymen would ride around and make hourly rounds to all the sentry posts throughout the camp, making sure the sentries were performing their duties.[^9] If they were not, Masculus would report this information back to his superiors.[^10] This vital role played by the cavalry is generally overlooked by most historians and the general public.

**Cavalry on the Battlefield**

> <img src="media/image6.png" style="width:2.54688in;height:3.39583in" />

Figure 5: Tomb monument of a cavalrymen from the 1st century AD, Didymus Ridgeland, [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tomb_monument_of_a_cavalryman_from_1st_century_AD_(Germanic_Roman_Museum_Cologne_Germany).jpg), [<u>CC ASA 4.0 I</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/deed.en)

By 66 AD, the cavalry had grown to become a core part of the Roman Legion, solidified by Augustus, who made cavalry a permanent part of the legion, with 120 cavalrymen.[^11] This cavalry detachment, including Masculus as a junior officer, would act as escorts, scouts, a battlefield unit, or relay communications.[^12] These roles were vital to and helped project Roman power through the provinces. As can be seen in the above image, Masculus would have likely carried both a sword and spear, similar to the Roman infantry, though his sword would have been specialized. He would have also carried a shield and wore a helmet similar to the one pictured below.

> <img src="media/image7.png" style="width:2.83854in;height:3.40324in" />

Figure 6: Cavalry Helmet from Witcham Gravel 1st Century AD, Michel Wal, [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Roman_helmet.jpg), [<u>CC ASA 1.0</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/1.0/deed.en)

On the battlefield, Masculus and his cavalry would have been organized on the flanks of the legion, on the wings or *alae*.[^13] As mentioned above, the cavalry would have performed reconnaissance duties for the legion prior to a battle. When the First Jewish War broke out in 66 AD, it is very likely that Masculus could have been sent with his cavalry on a scouting mission down to Judea from his camp in Syria, a journey that would have taken him about six days.[^14] This rapid movement by the cavalry would allow for an effective scouting of enemy displacements to be relayed back to the legions commanders to develop an effective battleplan. When on the battlefield, Masculus and his cavalry would either engage in flanking maneuvers or await the perfect time to strike. Towards the end of a battle, once the Legion had forced a retreat, the cavalry would begin to chase the retreating enemy away, turning it into a rout and removing all hope of reengaging.[^15] These tactics would prove effective in ensuring the success of the Roman army.

**Resistance: The First Jewish War**

> <img src="media/image2.png" style="width:1.95606in;height:2.60604in" />

Figure 7: Aerial view of Masada, Andrew Shiva, Wikipedia, [<u>CC BY-SA 4.0</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)

Resistance against the Roman Empire is seldom acknowledged in the realm of public entertainment. Occasionally, it can be seen in film (*Spartacus,* and *Gladiator* to a lesser extent), but the localized resistance of non-enslaved people against Roman rule is almost never seen in media. But it played a powerful role in the history of the Roman Empire, and its grasp on power. When the Romans would conquer a territory, they would generally keep a similar power structure in place with people loyal to Rome favored in that. This would be what was done in Judea, with the Jewish aristocracy given power in Judea to rule. Unfortunately for the Romans, these aristocrats were not very popular, and helped to sow the initial seeds of rebellion in the province of Judea.[^16]

Come 66 AD, the Jewish people were fed up with Roman rule. It all came to a head when Gessius Florus took money out of the temple in Jerusalem.[^17] This drove the Jews to revolt, forcing Florus out of town. They would destroy the Roman garrison in Jerusalem and at Masada[^18], the mountainside fortress pictured above. Masculus’ campaign would begin here. Following these actions by the Jews against the Roman Garrisons, Cestius Gallus, the General in Syria, would lead his armies south in an attempt to put down this rebellion, plundering along the way. Masculus would have likely ridden scout missions ahead of the Roman Army, looking for the Jewish Resistance, and likely failing to find them in some cases, as on Cestius’ march to Jerusalem he was attacked on the Sabbath (a Jewish day of rest) by the Jewish rebels. Cestius would arrive in Jerusalem and lay siege before breaking off and retreating, being followed out by the Jewish Rebels.[^19]

The future Emperor Vespasian would be sent by Nero to lead the armies converging on the Jewish Rebels. But before he could reach there to take command, a battle would take place where Masculus would have played a large role. The Jewish Rebels would launch a large attack against outnumbered Roman soldiers at Ascalon. On the battlefield, there was only an ala of cavalry and a cohort of Roman infantry.[^20] Masculus would likely have been one of them. Wave after wave of Jewish Rebels attacked, and each time the Roman cavalry would ride out and devastate their ranks, forcing them to retreat. Eventually the Jewish Rebels would give up and be forced to retreat back to Jerusalem in face of Vespasian’s arrival.[^21]

> <img src="media/image5.png" style="width:3.28646in;height:1.83283in" />

Figure 8: Arch of Titus Menorah, [<u>Steerpike</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/User:Steerpike), [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Arch_of_Titus_Menorah.png), [<u>CC A 3.0 U</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/deed.en)

Upon Vespasian’s arrival, he and his son Titus would rampage through the lands of Judea. Through all of this Masculus’ work as a scout would continue as he helped to guide the combined Roman Armies through their campaigns of reconquest. Eventually Vespasian would return to Rome to assume control as Emperor, and his son Titus would continue the campaign. Masculus and the Roman Army would march all the way back to the gates of Jerusalem, set up in battle array and begin a long siege of the city[^22] in 70 AD. The successful siege would lead to the plunder and destruction of the Temple of Solomon (seen above) and the war would end in 73 AD following the success of the Roman Army in subduing the final Jewish holdouts.

**Why this story matters**

> <img src="media/image8.png" style="width:3.59896in;height:2.72269in" />

Figure 9: Cavalry on Trajan’s Column, [<u>Joe Mabel</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/User:Jmabel), [<u>Wikimedia Commons</u>](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Trajan%27s_Column_-_NMR_-_panel_028_A.jpg), [<u>CC ASA 4.0 I</u>](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/deed.en)

The story of Masculus is important because of the lack of representation for Roman cavalrymen in media and modern writing. When writing about the Roman military, both in the modern day and in the Roman era, the role of the cavalry was severely underrepresented. Writings that detail much about the Roman cavalry are also few, and that where the evidence gaps make it hard to determine what it would have been like to be in the cavalry at this time. But it is important to understand the role the cavalry played in projecting the power of Rome across the empire. It is important to understand how these men lived, and to remember what their lives would have been like.

To understand how they would have viewed the Power of Rome, the Resistance to Rome, and the Roman Empire itself are all important.
