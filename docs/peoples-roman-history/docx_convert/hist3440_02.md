## A Household Servant:

An elite Roman household depended on enslaved labor and those who organized and carried out that labor held a complex position in the family and society. There were elite male heads of households who exercised legal authority over people who were considered property. There were also those who were enslaved to these elite men that were responsible for the daily domestic and sometimes supervisory tasks. There existed slaves like Felix, who was tasked with everyday household chores as well as having escorting duties for his master’s children.

For a slave like Felix, this role would have gone to a pedagogue, however with Felix being 12 years old he would be too young to be titled one. He acted as an escort for his master's children, escorting them to school and carrying their materials and ensuring that they arrived safely. It was a strange spot to put a slave into, being present in that educational setting but is legally excluded from them. He most likely would have overheard or even eavesdropped on elements being taught to the class. He also would have had domestic tasks aside from that ith cleaning, running errands and serving meals.

Felix’s position is further complicated within his role as he is the son of his master and an enslaved woman. Of course

## Roman Afterlives

The system that made Felix's life the way it was didn't go away as Rome transitioned. Roman customs of household hierarchy, inequality, and slavery had an impact on later societies throughout the world and especially in America

Modern depictions of Rome, particularly in films such as Gladiator, highlight spectacle, military might, and elite masculinity, while downplaying the realities of enslaved labor that made those lifestyles possible

Felix’s reality goes to show that Rome being idealized as a "great" civilization overlooks the reality of the role that slavery played in its system. This interpretation rethinks Roman society by focusing on a child who is biologically linked to power but can't get to it and looks at Roman society as one built not only on achievement, but on exploitation that was embedded and socially reinforced. Felix is both biologically tied to power and denied access to it.

## Mobility

At first glance, the chance for Felix to be traveling anywhere outside of Rome would be rare. But it's important to note for Felix because the act of traveling would never be his choice to make. Decimus, a wealthy landowner with strong ties to elite Roman society as well as Felix's master and biological father. Decimus is taking his claimed children to Pompeii after being invited by another elite household, and the trip is plausible in the context of the second-century roman world, where wealthy men traveled for social visits and for business. Pompeii also had an image of a prosperous trade city at the time. Using ORBIS, the route would be taken with a private carriage through the cities along the coast line before reaching Pompeii within 5 days.

Something to note is this trip means something different to each of the people travelling, for the children its a long relaxing vacation to a new place, for Decimus its a social and business trip a way to represent his status, and for Felix it's a job. As plain as it is, he's traveling because he is required to. He is a caretaker of his master's children and is treated as a tool on this vacation. And though he is protected by his master and or guards that may accompany them, he's not being protected because he is an individual but because he belongs to decimus and his status.

It's hard to say how Felix would have felt about the trip and by the large mobility in general. Felix may have found it exciting seeing new places. He was a household servant after all, getting away from the routine of household chores would cause some excitement but it doesn't erase the fact that the subconscious thought of him being labor would still be in his head. Talking about the negatives of a week-long trip, he wouldn't have much say over the conditions of the travel, and if illness had affected the children he would have some responsibility for that. ORBIS can tell us when and where people will travel to but it cannot capture the emotions of the passengers.Most evidence from this time comes from elites so we would have trouble accurately displaying how Felix understood this trip.

## Emotional and Social Hierarchy

Something we briefly touched on was the subconscious thought of Felix being labor always going through his head. Touching on that a bit more, the household in ancient Rome was defined by a constant awareness of power. And this hierarchy was visible in every interaction and enforced through discipline and through daily routines that reminded enslaved people of their position.

In Seneca's letter 47: on Master and slave we see a window into the thought processes of this relationship. Seneca describes how control was maintained through constant surveillance and punishment, “The slightest murmur is repressed by the rod; even a chance sound- a cough, a sneeze, or a hiccup- is visited with the lash”\[303\]. He also talks about how enslaved people were expected to remain physically and socially beneath their masters “when we recline at a banquet one slave mops up disgorged food, another crouches beneath the table and gathers up the left-overs of the tipsy guests \[305\].

For Felix, it would be hard to say that this structure of slavery wouldn’t have shaped his internal world, It would be plausible that he moved throughout his day with a heightened awareness of consequence. Subconsciously he would be asking himself after his every move if his behavior would warrant punishment. This self-regulation is something that was brought up in the writing of Epicteuts that says that a master “has power over your body,” not just physically but mentally. Resentment is also something to consider. Seneca writes “they are not enemies when we acquire them; we make them enemies” \[304\] We think it's plausible that Felix would have known that his master was his biological father, and seeing his half-sibling move freely through the household would plausibly cause tension

And maybe resentment wouldn't be the only emotion at play. Within a household defined by dependency and proximity it is also plausible that Felix desired approval or recognition from his father. As interesting as it would be to contemplate that the sources don't allow us to determine which of these feelings dominated if they existed at all. Again as before most sources talking about slaves are of elite men like Seneca who describe slavery but don't have the perspectives of the enslaved. Critical fabulation works within this gap so in Felix's case, the evidence says his life was shaped by discipline and emotional complexity.
