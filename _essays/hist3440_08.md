title: Perfume in the Roman Empire

Perfume is something most people know of in modern times, a way to show one’s character or promote confidence. In the Roman Empire though, it was everywhere. Perfume and scented oils were not just for aesthetics but also displayed wealth and hygiene of an individual or household, and it had uses in medicine, religion, and funerals. Perfume was both a signal of economic class and social class, the quality or scent of perfume was like jewelry in the way it symbolized what someone could afford and their taste in luxury goods. The ingredients, container types, and quality of production all played a part in how different perfumes were used in different aspects of society.

Claudia Helena, a fictional character who can be used to understand perfume in the Roman Empire, was the daughter of perfume makers who own their own shop in Pompeii. Their perfume was sold in the storefront on the street while it was produced in workshops in a connected building. Claudia, a seventeen-year-old unmarried Roman citizen, often helped out in her family’s shop and loved the perfume making process. Using Claudia as an example and as the perspective allows for a narrative about how perfume was made and used in the Roman Empire to form.

One of the downsides of ancient history is that sometimes time destroys evidence. Evidence from the Roman Empire exists on how perfume was made and the bottles they were stored in, but since these perfumes were natural materials there is very little evidence left about what was in these bottles. There are recipes written down, yes, and reasonable inferences can be made about the trade network of ingredients but the physical residue of perfume from the Roman Empire is very rare for archeologists and historians to come by.

## Perfume Production

Perfume is an art that has been around for thousands of years. The ingredients and process have changed as technology and society evolved. In the Roman Empire, the process for making perfume focused on techniques to make the scents stronger and last longer. Perfumes of the time were oil-based, commonly olive oil due to its quantity in the Mediterranean. Archeologist Jean-Pierre Brun explains the wedge press technique. Oil-soaked aromatic materials like flowers were wrapped in cloth and pressed with heavy stone machinery to extract the scented oils directly.[^1] Brun finds that pressed perfume was common in the ancient Mediterranean, but was not the only means used. Another common technique in Roman times was through infusion. The oil was heated gently and then aromatic ingredients were added into the warm oil for days or weeks at a time, infusing the oil with the scent; strength was based on how long and how fragrant the materials in the oil were. A great historical example of this process in Pompeii is from the House of Vettii, a prominent mansion that was well-preserved after the eruption of Mount Vesuvius.

{% include essay/image.html objectid="kay_001" caption="Eber, Johannes. “VI.15.1 East wall at south end, painted panel of cupids making perfumed oil.”" %}

In this fresco, small Cupid figures representing the perfume laborers who were most likely slaves in merchant households, shows the process from right to left of the perfume making process. The fresco shows a technique that was used in Pompeii and is direct evidence of the perfume making process. After the finished perfumes are bottled into either simple clay jars or decorative glass pieces depending on the quality and intended purpose, they are ready to be sold or used. The process of making perfume took time and focused on producing refined scents for their customers.

Another wonderful piece of perfume history that has survived time is the bottles of perfume. Many glass perfume bottles have been found by archeologists, giving an insight into the uses of perfume and places they would be found. This specific example of a glass perfume bottle shows a lot about the standard glass bottles that the character Claudia Helena would’ve interacted with daily.

{% include essay/image.html objectid="kay_002" caption="Ojeda, R.G. “VI.9.1 Pompeii. Glass perfume bottle.”" %}

This bottle from Pompeii in its current state has a slight iridescent sheen from where it might have been painted with decorations. Other bottles found were more decorative and used for art as much as storage. Glass was not the only material used in the storage of perfume though. Glass is difficult to make and shape, so glass bottles for perfume were often used for higher quality perfumes or special occasions. More commonly, ceramic unguentarium or clay jars were used in a higher variety of products.

One aspect of Claudia Helena’s life that is hard to discern from evidence alone is how her position as a woman might have affected the way she could interact with her family’s perfume business. What role did class play when it came to making perfume? How did women interact with the process? Using two frescos as evidence and the character Claudia Helena, a critical fabulation forms about how she as the daughter of a perfume shop family may have directly interacted with perfume and the process of making it.

{% include essay/image.html objectid="kay_003" caption="Chappell, Stephen. “Fresco detail depicting a woman pouring perfume into a bottle.”" %}

Claudia Helena was born a Roman citizen in a family that owned a perfume shop in Pompeii. Behind the shop was a small garden where local ingredients like roses and olives could be grown, but also the production space where household slaves would primarily work to make and bottle the perfume that would be sold in the storefront that touched the public street. Most ingredients used in luxury perfumes were brought in through trade though, like patchouli and spices from India or flowers from Egypt. Claudia’s family perfume shop didn’t focus on just one scent but catered to a wide market of different scents. As a consumer as much as a merchant, Claudia often enjoyed experimenting with new ingredients from a young age. Despite her interest in the process, she didn’t physically take part in the production due to her status as the daughter of the family. Slaves would handle the heavy labor of pressing scented ingredients with olive oil to extract the scent, boiling it down to an applicable perfume, and then bottling the final product into artistically created glass perfume bottles.

In a fresco from the Villa Farnesina in Rome, a well-dressed and likely high class woman is seen pouring contents into a glass bottle that resembles the glass perfume bottles found in archaeology.[^2] What Claudia may have occasionally taken a part in is the bottling of perfume, transferring the scented oils into decorative bottles for herself or the store like the Villa Farnesina fresco depicts. As seen in the Villa Farnesina fresco, women of status or wealth like Claudia were not above this action. While she did not handle the labor side of perfume making, Claudia Helena was not removed from the process and interested in the experimental side of using foreign ingredients and creating new scent combinations.

Mobility is an important aspect of life in the Roman Empire. There were no cars or planes to move people around, so traveling was not only slower but also more complicated. Claudia Helena, being a young, unmarried woman, would not have been able to travel alone but needed to travel with family or under the care of a patroness. However, her citizenship would not have limited her as much as it would have if she were a slave. Pompeii was a thriving market hub, but not as much as Rome was. A plausible journey that Claudia would have taken with her family would be to Rome to visit relatives. The most efficient way that they would have traveled to Rome from Pompeii would start by ship, usually through a deal made with a merchant ship as chartered boats were unheard of and only the wealthiest had their own private ships solely for personal travel. The closest port to Rome is the city of Ostia, about a two day sail from Pompeii according to the data from “ORBIS.”[^3] Since passage on a ship was often bartered, an average price for the amount of time spent traveling would be around twenty sestertius or four to five denarii. Once in Ostia though, Claudia and her family would continue to Rome by wagon, a little over a day away and around ten sestertius for the wagon and the price of wheat for the donkeys pulling it.[^4] This three-day journey may seem simple to modern people used to convenience, but it was difficult for people of the Roman Empire physically and financially. The thirty sestertius just for the one way journey to Rome, much less food and board or returning to Pompeii, was expensive especially for merchants such as Claudia Helena’s family.

## Uses of Perfume

Perfume was a part of everyday life in the Roman Empire. It was used for personal scents like it is in modern times, for both men and women, and became synonymous with a person's presence. Poets like Catullus used lines like “sweet perfumes in her mother’s gentle embrace” to show the emotional connection with which memory and perfume scents were associated.[^5] Scented oils, which were less distilled than typical perfume for a heavier and moisturizing feel, were also highly associated with hygiene, particularly as a sign of wealth and class. It would be used in women’s hair, after bathing, and around the body to display decadence. Regular perfume was also used in these spaces and with furniture and clothing. The ability to buy large amounts of perfume and oils needed to incorporate it into so many places showed wealth in Roman society. However, perfume was not limited as a luxury. While certain ingredients were used in higher quality, and therefore more expensive, products there were a variety of types of perfumes for different uses. One of those ways was medicine. Remedies and most forms of medical care were based in nature and plants were associated with the different medicinal properties.

Practitioners like Dioscorides wrote on the uses of different plants and oils, and the benefits of these plants were similarly paired with perfumes using those ingredients. For example, one of the entries in Dioscorides’ *De Materia Medica* is for the rose or rhodides plant. He writes that roses could help with headaches, bruising, and a plethora of other things depending on how they were prepared.[^6] These healing traits were also associated with rose based perfume, connecting the aesthetic qualities of the scent in the perfume with the intended medicinal properties. While roses were commonly grown on Italian soil, many of the ingredients in medicine and perfume came from around the world.

There is quite a lot of material evidence for perfume from the Roman Empire. From frescos and bottles to archeological remnants. One completed project in the excavation of Pompeii was the discovery of what is believed to have been a perfume garden, where fragrant florals and fruits were grown as experiments and samples for perfume. The discovery and recreation of this garden using plant fossils show that garden spaces were present inside the city and may have been large enough for small-scale production. The archeologists found evidence of roses, cherry trees, and violets which were all commonly used in perfume, but also terracotta and glass bottles that date to perfume production during the 3rd century BCE.[^7] This space wasn’t just decorative, but used to grow flora for the experimentation of scents for perfumes.

{% include essay/image.html objectid="kay_004" caption="Anderson, Sonja. “The 2,000-Year-Old ‘Perfume Garden’ in the Ancient City of Pompeii Has Been Restored to Its Former Glory.”" %}

Like the Roman Empire itself, the perfume market was widespread. The trade networks of the Roman Empire were a big influence on the popular ingredients and trends in perfume. The primary trade was that of aromatics, “mainly incense, myrrh, and oriental spices”[^8] Since perfume was used in a variety of different ways, the strength of the aroma was one of the key factors that customers looked for. Higher-class and wealthier people often used the scent and strength of their perfume as indicators of the quality they were able to afford. Pompeii had a diverse economic system, connected to the trade routes around the Mediterranean, local agricultural production, and an urban environment filled with numerous shops.[^9] This created the perfect environment for merchant shops to thrive, like perfume. Ingredients could be grown outside the city, brought in through trade, and then produced in the workshops that would be behind the shop. The urban environment of Pompeii also allowed for different economic classes to be able to access shops like the perfume shop owned by Claudia Helena’s family instead of the shop catering to a specific class. This means that they would also have been selling products to both the upper-class of Pompeii and to the middle class who would still use perfume in their everyday lives through the variety of ingredients they would have to work with through trade and local production.

## Conclusion

Perfume may seem materialistic in today’s world, but it was a part of culture in the Roman Empire. The ingredients and uses of perfume encompassed many aspects of everyday life, from cosmetics to medicine. We can see the trade network, technique, and social experiences that the Romans lived in. By understanding the importance of perfume in people’s lives, we can also gain an understanding of who they were as human beings.
