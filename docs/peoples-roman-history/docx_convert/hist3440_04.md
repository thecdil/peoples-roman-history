Narrative Essay Format Gaius Primus Valerius and the Uncertainty of Life of a Night Watchman

Character: Gaius Valerius Primus, age 34, Roman citizen(acquired through service). Rome, Italy, 1st century CE.

Theme: Violence, Spectacle, and Control

In the barracks of the Cohortes Vigilum every man has his own place and position. The Vigiles were well aware of their position as both the safety net of the city and enforcers of the regime. They were ready and willing to use any means necessary to discharge their dual duties. They carried trudgeons for control and would be ready with axes in the case of fire (Daugherty). Gaius Primus Valerius, one such man, did these things. Perhaps he was motivated to protect his family and his place in the world. Maybe he enjoyed what he did and thought of the control maintained as a positive good. This paper is not primarily about the axes and pumps that the Vigiles used. It instead examines how men like Valerius worked to maintain the status quo (<span class="mark">Polybius)</span>.

A Position of Control

Guarding the streets and ensuring order would always be an important concern of the empire. The vigiles were the first line of defense in keeping control amid a constantly shifting set of public opinions. The vigiles had a hierarchical command structure at the top was the Prefectus Vigiles whose job it was to oversee the seven cohorts each of 1,000 men, each cohort was commanded by a tribune and in turn split into 10 centuries each with 100 men and led by a centurion (Bakker). Gaius was near the bottom of this arrangement, just another man in a sea of thousands. That was only relative to his superiors within his organization though. He held a notable amount of power over the average inhabitant of Rome by virtue of his station.

The control established by the vigiles was seen as civic responsibility and had an element of religious significance. The control exerted by the nightwatchmen like Gauis was not entirely brute force. Records of the vigiles tell us that they were mainly known for their fire fighting capabilities(Rainbird). It leaves out much of their day to day interactions with the citizenry. These men would have known their place as guards against disorder and disobedience to the social order. They knew how to properly manage a city and a people to keep order without becoming simple bludgeoning tools themselves.

This inscription shows the feriae of the vigiles: it focuses on their tools and actions instead of anything about them as people. Most records of the Vigiles are concerned about how they did their jobs and neglects why they did them (Suetonius).

What the Evidence Shows and what it Obscures.

Gaius Valerius Primus never existed. He never walked the streets of Rome or threw water at a burning building. He is not real. But he is useful, the written record does not remember the people like Valerius when we want to take a closer look at the time when people very much like the fictional Valerius. While taking the idea of a 1st century man and responsibly reasoning what his life may have looked like we can construct a likely picture based on the historical evidence that is provided to us.

When it comes to a man like the one created for this exercise there’s a lot we don’t know. We have little information how he interacted with his supervisors or how he would talk with the inhabitants of Rome. The records show the vigiles being mentioned with the occasional tombstone showing years of service, name, rank, etc… It shows what they did but not always how they did it. The focus also skews heavily towards the actions and responsibility of the Prefectus Vigilum neglecting the more common members (Gardner). We don’t have the actions of individuals.

We should pause to take a look at the composition of the vigiles originally the force was made up of state slaves. Eventually however this was changed and the force became made primarily of noncitizens seeking citizenship after a period of 6 years. The institution was founded on the use of enslaved persons and evolved into one that used the carrot of social advancement to entice recruits (Canter). This would have affected the way the people both outside and inside the vigiles looked at the group whether they realized it or not.

Moving through Rome: Why Mobility Matters

Mobility is so important in ancient Rome because the access to it varies so much. A person's ability to choose where they and their family resided shows us a lot about how this society worked. Mobility ended up being reliant on social capital, economic privilege, gender, and access to infrastructure. The way a singular person moves or is rendered unable to move can tell us much and more about how the broader society and specifically those near the top see them.

Rome was a city massive in many respects in the 1st century C.E. It had an estimated population of around 1 million (Cassius). Rome had become a place where resources like grain, linen, silk, slaves, and other exotic goods flowed into Rome. These things came from as far west as modern day Portugal or as far east as what is today Syria. Meanwhile Rome exported their own culture, expectation, and system of government to a sometimes unwilling world. The vigiles were a necessary part in maintaining this system. Their own movements were somewhat limited mostly from their own homes to where they would patrol but they enabled movement within the city in the darker hours by providing a layer of safety to a dangerous nighttime environment. There was one set of goods that the vigiles did occasionally need to transport themselves. They would sometimes be sent to work somewhere else for example Ostia using the ORBIS route modeling tool which constructs what Roman travel ways would have looked like. We can see that going there from Rome would have taken .4 days and going back would have taken .8. The difference is due to one way being downstream and the other being upstream. This distance given the context that it was not a common trip to be made was manageable for my character. The cost would be less than a denarius (*ORBIS)*.

It would be deceptive however to see this as the whole story. Any move such as this would have a price paid in losses. There would be the lost security of support systems. The control that Gaius was used to exercising would change from into something new in a different place. His actions and presentation would be scrutinized in a way they were not at home. His lack of control in this new place is representative of the lack of control many others had in their own lives, he, relatively speaking, was autonomous. He would be in any new place an outsider. The Roman system of control operated off of people keeping each other in line with a constant undercurrent of fear. The way they saw disobedience as a transgression against both the state and by extension the gods themselves helped to justify this limiting of personal freedoms. There are limits to the tools we have before us ORBIS shows us estimates of what could happen. It does not show us what would be necessary to make these things happen, two people can both be equal in wealth but the movements of one will be stifled by their other statuses whether it be their gender, citizenship status, or anything else.

Control on Patrol

The remaining marks the vigiles have left on the world in the form of graffiti and the tools we can see that they used. The whips, and cudgels that they carried showed how they were ready to punish and control at a moment's notice. It lets us see their capacity for punishment, perhaps even unilateral beatings (Fuhrmann). They could have seen this as a duty or simply have wished to use whatever power they had. The firefighting tools show a side of the organization that was focused on managing nonhuman threats.

Walking the streets was not simply an expression of a unit that intended to keep the city safe. As much as this controlled crime it also controlled those with no ill intent. The presence of armed men on the street at night paid for by the emperor was a message. It told people that the long arm of the state was everywhere they went in Rome.

It is impossible to say with certainty how any member of the vigiles would have seen their own position. We know from scattered records of other people in similar but not identical positions that they likely developed relationships, even friendships, with those whose alleyways and roads they walked. The areas that the biggest blanks occupy have to do with how they saw themselves. Were they protectors of the people or were they enforcers of the law?

This is an opportunity for us to use the tool that is critical fabulation to make responsible statements about what seems likely. A man like Gaius who was on the streets every day probably knew much about the people along his route. He could have had a good sense of whose insula was most dangerous if it were to burn due to excessive and unsafe additions (Rainbird). He could never have known every criminal in Rome, but he might have recognized beggars and known which ones were likely to cause him trouble and which would not. He would have felt the control that he exerted on the lives of others even if he couldn’t put a name to it.

Why this Story Matters Now

When we most often think of Rome we think of grandeur, scope, and chaos. We see political infighting and conquered nations. This is not the totality of Rome however. For much of its history, especially the first and second centuries the imperial core was a place relatively untouched by largescale violence. Thus the works of people who lived in these times go under the radar. The lives of the Vigiles are in the relative scale of these things well documented even if there’s so much we don’t know.

The lack of detail on this comes down to the biases of those who give us our sources. The day to day encounters of the people escaped the historical record while elites were writing about what they cared about. To better understand the lives of men like Gaius or really most inhabitants of ancient times we need to look at sources with an eye for what is left out. What can we find out when we look at what was thought about service, control, and the actions of the masses (<span class="mark">Fuhrmann)</span>.

Gaius Valerius Primus is a work of our imaginations, but he comes in part from things that really happened. He is a piece of what made Rome into Rome. He helps us answer the question of maintaining control and public safety, of the difference between justice and mere punishment. How do we look at the records when we are trying to better understand the people that helped maintain systems that simultaneously had great capacity for cruelty, order, and their society as a whole.
